The brief

Uninspired and kind of bare

That was the description, and it is accurate, but the cause is not what it looks like. The plants in this bed are healthy and correctly chosen. The hydrangeas are happy in full southern sun, the boxwoods are alive, the rhododendron is dense to the ground. Nothing here is failing.

What is missing is arrangement and layers. The shrubs sit in an even checkerboard that reads as neither formal nor natural, and beneath them there is nothing at all: no ground layer, no seasonal layer, no spring. A bed of evenly spaced shrubs over bare mulch will read as bare no matter how good the shrubs are.

So this is not a rip-out. Every existing shrub stays and gets relocated, one plant leaves, and the two missing layers get added. The bones here are sound. The arrangement is the problem.
The organizing idea

The porch already tells the bed what to do

Five white columns divide the front of the house into regular bays. It is the strongest geometry on the property and the current planting ignores it completely. Align everything to those bays, hold every mass at a consistent height, and most of the "uninspired" problem is solved before a single new plant arrives. This bed does not need more variety. It needs order.

  • Hydrangeas relocate into bay-aligned groups against the skirt, where they hide the white beadboard and pick up the rhythm of the columns above them.
  • Boxwoods group into mounded punctuation between those bays instead of marching evenly across the bed. This also does something practical: grouping them off a solid skirt gets air moving around them, which matters in a county where boxwood blight is a live risk.
  • The golden mop cypress comes out. Against cream shingle and white columns, its chartreuse reads as an accident rather than a choice. It is the one plant that fights the house.
  • The rhododendron anchors the far end and stays exactly as it is.
Two vantages, two compositions. From the street this bed is read in elevation, against the white skirt. From the porch chairs, three feet above it, it is read looking straight down. That second view is why the new palette leans on flat-topped flowers and fine texture: they are what hold up seen from above, and most foundation plantings ignore this entirely.
The palette

Fewer plants, bigger masses, stronger forms

Click any photo to see it large. The move here is restraint. Six kinds of plant across thirty feet, each one repeated in large blocks rather than dotted through, and every one chosen for its shape first and its flower second. Nothing wispy, nothing that reads as a meadow. The color that does appear is drawn straight from the winter containers already at this door: bronze, plum, dusty rose, cream.

What is deliberately not here. No fine-textured or airy perennials, no yarrow, no meadow grasses, no soft flowering groundcover. Those plants are lovely and they belong to a different kind of house. This elevation is crisp, pale and symmetrical, and it wants planting with weight and edges rather than haze.

April opens with apricot-cupped daffodils and plum fritillaria spires. Rhododendron carries May, allium spheres float through June, hydrangea cones run July into September, and the sedum closes out in dusty rose while the liriope flowers below it. In winter the dried cones, the rust sedum plates, the evergreen liriope band and the boxwood are all still standing. The seven arcs on the cover are these same windows drawn as a chart.

An honest note about who gets fed. This is a formal entry bed and its first job is how the house reads from the street, so its ecological contribution is modest by design. Allium and sedum are both genuinely excellent forage, and the rhododendron gives May, but panicle hydrangea offers little and the daffodils none. The real habitat work belongs elsewhere on the property, and the recommendation is the shaded strip along the side and rear of the house, currently bare gravel. That is a separate design and a good one.

It gives the entry a focal point the bed does not have to provide, and it means the planting itself can stay calm.

Installation

Bulbs this fall, everything else in spring

  1. This fall: plant the bulbs. They go in ahead of everything else and they are the reason the bed has an April at all.
  2. Early spring, while everything is still dormant: the crew moves the hydrangeas and boxwoods into their new positions, and the golden mop cypress comes out. Dormant relocation is why this happens in March rather than May.
  3. Mark the bays first. Before anything is dug, stand in the street and check the groups against the columns. This alignment is the whole design; it is worth ten minutes of looking.
  4. Then the perennials, back to front: the sedum blocks first, then the liriope band last so you are not stepping over it. Set the liriope with a taut string line. A wandering edge undoes the whole effect.
  5. Plant at grade. Hole twice the width of the pot, exactly its depth, and water each plant as it goes in rather than all at the end.
  6. Mulch two inches, never against stems. Within two seasons the geranium closes and the mulch becomes unnecessary, which is the point.
The irrigation needs a decision before planting. This bed is on drip, and drip waters points rather than areas. A continuous ground layer planted between existing emitters gets almost nothing. Either the line is extended with additional emitters through the ribbon, or the geranium gets hand-watered through its first summer and is left to fend for itself after that, which it can do once established. The first option is better and it is a small job while the bed is open.

No deadheading required, no staking, no spraying. Watch the boxwoods for blight in humid stretches now that they have air around them, and keep the fallen leaves cleared from beneath them, which is the single most useful preventive habit.
Open items

Four things to confirm

  1. A tape on the bed. The run and depth are scaled from photographs. Quantities move with the real numbers, particularly the geranium ribbon.
  2. The drip decision: extend the line through the ground layer, or hand-water the first summer.
  3. Rhododendron flower color. It has only been seen out of bloom. If it reads hot pink or magenta rather than soft, it wants either relocating or company that can absorb it.
  4. Growing-season photographs. Every photo on file is dormant-season. A few in leaf would confirm the bay alignment and settle the rhododendron question at the same time.
