The brief

Two good plants and thirty feet of nothing

What was there: a magnificent variegated hosta anchoring the gate corner, healthy silver Brunnera at the door end, and between them thirty feet of bare mulch and wandering vinca. It read as inherited foundation planting rather than a design, on the stretch of the property everyone walks past every day.

The bed wraps the outside corner of the house in two legs, from the gate around the window bay to the side door and its brick landing. Granite Belgian block against the driveway, brick underfoot, white clapboard above. Good bones, poor planting.

Conditions that shaped every choice. Morning sun, shade by afternoon, so this is a part-shade bed. Deer are excluded by the property's own barriers but rabbits and woodchucks are not, so the filter here is rabbit-resistance rather than deer-resistance. Full irrigation on this zone. Low maintenance is a requirement, not a preference. No ornamental grasses anywhere on this property.

This is a bee bed, honestly. Butterflies want sun and open composites, and a shaded entry cannot offer them; what it can offer is early forage when queen bumblebees are the only things flying. April opens with bluebells for those queens and daffodils against the clapboard. The geranium ribbon runs pink through May and June. July is quiet on purpose, carried by hosta scapes over silver, gold and lime. Then cream plumes from August to frost, and the inkberry holds the winter. The seven arcs on the cover are these same windows, in these same colors, drawn as a chart.

Installation

One weekend, plus an hour in October

Saturday is shopping and layout, Sunday is digging. Bulbs wait for late October.

Before you start

  1. Take the before photos: both legs, from the gate, from the door, one wide from the driveway.
  2. Test the soil pH where the two shrubs will land. Inkberry wants acid soil and mortar leaches lime; anything much above 6.5 is worth a conversation before the shrubs go in.
  3. Lay the plants out in their pots and live with the arrangement for a few hours before anything gets dug.

Clearing

  1. Pull the vinca. Fork under the mats, lift, then chase the runners, since it roots at every node. Perfection is not the goal; you will spot-pull stragglers next spring.
  2. Vinca goes in the trash, never the compost. It re-roots from fragments.
  3. Pull the old path light, its stake, and its wire. Nothing replaces it.
  4. Spread one to two inches of compost over the cleared soil. Do not dig it in; planting will do that.

Planting

  1. Shrubs first, then heuchera, astilbe and brunnera, then the geranium divisions. Water each plant as you set it, not all at the end.
  2. Hole twice the width of the pot, exactly the depth of the pot. The root flare sits at grade. Planting too deep is the most common way a good shrub dies slowly.
  3. Geranium divisions: the thick rhizomes run along the surface. Cut sections with roots attached, lay them down, barely cover the rhizome. The easiest division in gardening.
  4. Two inches of mulch everywhere except against stems and crowns. In two seasons the geranium is the mulch.
No fertilizer, at all. Fall feeding pushes soft growth straight into frost. Compost is the entire soil program here. If anything looks pale next May, a light compost top-dress is the whole toolkit.

Late October, one hour

Fifty 'Thalia' in the four dashed drifts, five inches deep, pointy end up. Five Mertensia roots in their drift beside the corner. No bone meal, since it mostly advertises the bulbs to whatever digs. Water once, then done.

The nothing list. No deadheading, ever. No staking. No spraying. No fall cleanup beyond the hosta. No watering after the first year. April, May, August, September and October you walk past and enjoy it.

Year one only

  • Watch the inkberry leaves next June. Yellowing between green veins means the pH conversation was needed after all.
  • Spot-pull vinca regrowth as it appears. First season only; the geranium closes ranks by season two.
  • Hand-water the new shrubs in a rainless stretch through the first summer.
  • The geranium divisions may sulk for a few weeks after moving. They are bombproof.
Open items

What is still unresolved

Soil pH at the shrub positions. The only test left before buying. Inkberry is genuinely acid-loving and this bed sits against a foundation, where mortar can push soil alkaline. Above roughly 6.5 and the honest options are to acidify annually, which contradicts the low-maintenance brief, or to choose a pH-indifferent structure plant instead. Below that and the shrubs go in as drawn.
The brunnera is bought, not divided. The existing clumps went in early last summer, so they are one season old. Dividing a plant that young sets both halves back and risks losing them; brunnera wants two or three seasons to build the root mass that makes division free. Four new plants now, and the whole group becomes a division source in 2028.
The astilbe is an addition, not part of the original plan. Three were added after the summer-color gap was flagged twice independently. They sit on the deep leg where there is room. If the bed reads busy next July, they are the first thing to move.
